# United States v. SSG Saintloth: case feedback analysis

**Basis and limits.** Built from two documents only: the defense slide deck ("Details for DFE with messages") and the Preliminary Hearing Officer (PHO) report of 10 Aug 2026. Not reviewed: the interview video, the full chat extraction, the ad, any forensic report. I am not a lawyer and this is not legal advice. Statements of law are my understanding, and defense counsel should confirm each one.

**Updated 23 Nov 2026.** Later sections take further material in the order it was supplied. Section 8: the deck's timeline slide and one interview statement, as relayed and not as heard on the video. Section 9: the two-day timeline and the "Ok. Where at?" point. Section 10: the single account of May 1 the defense then put forward. Section 11: three further points, and whether the image search counts as a disclosure that she was an adult. Section 12: a college email posted on the profile, and how the undercover number appeared in the texting app. Section 13: his responses to points in this analysis, including that he had meant to block the number. Section 14: the provider's help pages, and the record of the habit. Section 1 gives the current position. Sections 2 to 7 have been revised where later material changes them.

## 1. Bottom line

*Current as of section 14.*

**The charges**

- **Spec 3** (attempted patronizing, as amended) is strong for the government, and age is irrelevant to it. **Spec 4** should fall away: the PHO found no probable cause and the government said it would dismiss.
- **Specs 1 and 2** turn on one question: on May 1, did he believe the person he was texting was under 16? There was no real child, so these are attempts, and an attempt is judged on the facts as the accused believed them. "Should have realized" is not the test. In the excerpts provided, age is never mentioned on May 1.

**The defense account as it now stands**

1. On April 30 he was told she would be 16 in June and refused within 37 seconds. He searched the ad's photo and found an adult's profile, which states an age of 23 and carries a posted college email from 2021. The phone records the profile being viewed at 16:54, eleven minutes after the conversation ended. He says the search was started during it.
2. He meant to block her number. He understood that the app lifts a block once a message is sent to the blocked number, which the provider's help pages confirm (section 14.1). So he looked at other texting apps to avoid messaging her by accident, and stayed with the one he had. He gave no time for this. The phone records a look at other apps at 14:01 to 14:04 on May 1.
3. At 15:49 he meant to pick up a conversation with another woman from earlier that afternoon. The undercover number was one of several unsaved numbers listed in the app, and he opened the wrong conversation. He does not recall seeing the April 30 messages.
4. The location she gave was not the one he expected. He suspected a scam and asked for pictures.
5. A second picture led him to take her for the woman on the profile, an adult. He did not connect the profile with April 30.
6. What he told the agents that evening was, as I read his response, a generalization from how he usually thinks or acts, and it left out these details (section 13.2).

**What supports it**

- The refusal. It is the government's own exhibit, and it shows what he did the one time he believed she was a minor.
- Nothing was said about age on May 1.
- Two records made before the arrest: the profile views on April 30, and the look at other texting apps at 14:01 to 14:04 on May 1 (sections 13.2 and 13.5). They show what he did. What he meant by it still comes from him.
- The profile states an age in writing, and a dated college record supports it. That its owner is an adult is close to beyond dispute (sections 12.1 and 13.3).
- The mistake has an ordinary mechanism: conversations listed by number only, and brought back whenever the app was reinstalled (sections 12.2 and 14.2).
- The provider's help pages bear out what he said about blocking in this app (section 14.1).
- As relayed, his Google activity shows that removing the app was his routine before April 30. If so, the removals say nothing about her (section 14.3).
- His first messages on May 1 read more naturally as continuing an arrangement than as returning to someone he had refused (section 10.1).

**What stands against it**

- On his own account he had singled out her number that afternoon, less than two hours before he texted it if the app search marks the moment. Her conversation was still in his list, though deleting it would have removed it for good (sections 13.5 and 14.2).
- On his own answers, and on the provider's description of the app, the April 30 exchange was in the conversation he opened (sections 12.2, 13.4 and 14.2).
- The interview statement, as relayed, treats her as one person on both days (sections 8.2 and 13.2).
- The profile's age is hers only if he believed its owner was the person texting, and until the second photo he doubted the pictures were real (sections 11.3 and 13.3).
- The account needs him to have remembered the profile and forgotten why he looked it up (section 11.2).
- What he did at the hotel. The removal of the apps minutes after he described his car counts for less if removal was his routine, but its timing still draws questions (sections 9.3, 13.1 and 14.3).
- Several points have now been given in more than one version (sections 8.3 and 13.6).

**What it still asks a fact-finder to accept on his word**

1. That, having worried about texting that number by accident, he then opened its conversation at 15:49 without noticing whose it was or reading the lines above the box.
2. That he remembered the profile but not the reason for the search.
3. That what he told the agents that evening was not what he had thought.

Each is possible. Needing all three is the difficulty. Records can take some of the weight off the first, and the video may do the same for the third.

**What will decide it**

1. The interview video: the exact words, and what he had been told before he said them.
2. What the texting app displayed at 15:49, and what he sent in the other woman's conversation after that time (sections 12.2 and 13.4). The provider's copy of that conversation may no longer exist (section 14.2).
3. Whether any record from before the arrest shows why he looked at other apps (sections 13.5 and 14.1).
4. The second photo and the advertisement (sections 11.2 and 11.3).

**Texas.** The offense there can rest on what he was told, so the search may not bear on guilt (section 11.4).

**Urgent.** The provider says a number lapses after 30 days without use, and that message history may not survive long inactivity. Nearly seven months have passed. Counsel should be asked now whether the provider's records were preserved or obtained, by the defense or by the government (section 14.2). The profile and the Google account's activity can also be lost or changed (sections 11.5 and 13.7).

## 2. Prosecution

### Strengths

1. **Age was disclosed and understood.** "i will be 16 in june is that ok" drew "No. Find a different way of life please." He cannot claim he missed it.
2. **He initiated the return.** Same UC number, same thread, about 23 hours later. Nobody lured him back. On the defense's present account he opened that thread by mistake, which still puts the April 30 exchange in front of him (section 12.2).
3. **May 1 shows intent to complete a paid encounter.** He offered an Uber, answered "Ok. Where at?" 18 seconds after "2 for 1 tonight 100," sent "Yes" and "Just you….then both," asked for pictures, drove about 20 minutes, asked "Cash right?", asked to be walked upstairs, wrote "I have tinted windows if you can carplay. Ill give extra," and identified his vehicle. Travel to the meeting place is a textbook substantial step.
4. **Concealment evidence, some of it supplied by the defense deck itself.** Tinted windows, nervousness, the peace-sign verification request, burner-number app searches, removal of the texting app in the parking lot, and a pre-arrival search for sting indicators. He has since said the habit predates these events and has given a reason for the app searches (sections 13.1, 13.5 and 14).
5. **A recorded interview after a signed rights waiver.** DPS reports he "admitted to knowingly speaking to a minor," and he admitted prior patronizing. The relayed statement that he "thought the next day she just needed money" supplies the link the texts lack: that on May 1 he knew who he was texting (section 8).

### Weaknesses

1. **No ad in the evidence packet.** The government told the PHO he answered an ad for an underage child. The PHO rejected that for lack of evidence and found he did not know the age until told.
2. **Nothing about age on May 1.** The UC never re-confirmed age in the conversation that matters. The PHO found only that he "had reason to believe" she was 15, which is a probable-cause finding and not proof beyond a reasonable doubt of actual belief.
3. **The April 30 refusal is the government's own exhibit.** It was immediate and moral. The PHO's word "hesitated" undersells it.
4. **On May 1 the explicit words are the UC's.** His are "Yes," "Just you….then both" and "carplay." "An amazing bj" predates the age disclosure. Spec 1 (indecent language) depends on reading short replies in context.
5. **The "Yes" timing.** It posted 2 seconds after "on both of us lol" and 24 seconds after "ok u gonna cum," which followed his own "Its 20 min away from me." A "you gonna come?" reading is available.
6. **The PHO's intent reasoning is thin.** It infers specific intent "because" the conduct was a substantial step, and it treats the $50 agreement as if it came after the age disclosure. It came before, and he withdrew. At trial, intent must be proved on its own.
7. **The DPS summary of the "admission" is second-hand.** It comes from an agent who was not in the room, and the PHO, who watched the video, cited it only for prior patronizing "but not minors" and for marital status. If nothing on the video says he knew or believed on May 1 that she was 15, the summary overstates. This is a smaller weakness than it first appeared, because the relayed statement lets the government argue the same point from his own words (section 8).
8. **Polygraph evidence is inadmissible (M.R.E. 707),** which complicates how the interview is presented.

## 3. Defense

### Strengths

1. **The 37-second refusal.** It is direct evidence of what he does when he knows the age.
2. **The legal standard.** The government must prove what he believed, and its proof for May 1 is inference. Section 11.3 sets out the standard and one open question about it.
3. **Everything he saw was adult.** By his own search, the photos trace to a 23-year-old's account, and the PHO found his history was with adults, "not minors." The profile also carried a posted college email from 2021 (section 12.1).
4. **Digital corroboration, if it holds.** The image search 11 minutes after the April 30 chat ended; other profiles messaged on May 1 before the UC (the two-day timeline lists five between 13:48 and 15:30, all still awaiting forensic confirmation); hotel lookups in Round Rock and Georgetown at 3:18 PM, half an hour before the UC text; the same stock opener to other numbers ("Are you still in killeen?", "Hi are you still available?").
5. **An innocent reading of the caution.** Paying an adult for sex is also a crime and a career-ender for an NCO. Nervousness, deletion and tinted windows do not distinguish between the two.
6. **The PHO's recommendation.** A neutral judge advocate recommended a bad-conduct discharge with no confinement. It is non-binding, but it is leverage in negotiation.

### Weaknesses

1. **Too many theories, and they fight each other.** A panel that hears all of them is likely to believe none. Section 8.3 explains this in full. Section 10 tests the single account the defense has since put forward.
   - "I didn't realize it was the same person" conflicts with "my image search told me she was an adult." The second means he recognized the ad he had researched right after being told 15.
   - "The photos showed an adult" conflicts with "I knew the photos were fake." Fake photos say nothing about who is texting.
   - "I did not intend to meet anyone" conflicts with the drive, "Cash right?", "Can you walk me upstairs?" and "Ill give extra."
   - Three separate misreadings ("on" as "or," "will" as "we'll," "Ok" answering only "we have a room") look contrived when stacked.
2. **The linchpin is unproven.** The deck says he "can't definitively remember" deleting the April 30 thread. If the extraction shows the thread was still there at 15:49:15 on May 1, the refusal exchange sat directly above the box he typed in. Uninstalling the app cannot explain the mistake either, since the UC log shows the May 1 text arriving in the same thread from the same sender. The defense now says the thread was in the app's list and he opened it by mistake (section 12.2). That makes what the screen showed the central forensic question. The provider says a deleted conversation cannot be recovered, so on that account the thread had not been deleted (section 14.2).
3. **His diligence cuts against him.** A man who reverse-searches photos, demands verification and checks for stings is hard to believe when he says he did not notice a number from 23 hours earlier. This has less force if, as now relayed, the conversations were listed by number only (section 12.2).
4. **The image search began within minutes of the age disclosure.** Expect: "You were checking whether she was really 15, looking for a reason to say yes." He now says it was started during the conversation (section 13.4).
5. **"Compulsion" serves the government's story too:** the urge overrode Thursday's "no." It is mitigation for sentencing, not a defense to intent.
6. **Self-inflicted exhibits.** Searches for second-number apps at 2:01–2:04 PM on May 1, including a visit to "Vikk – AI Lawyer & Legal Help" at 2:02 PM; the claimed pre-arrival sting search; removal of the texting apps at 17:02, minutes after he described his car. Each needs a truthful explanation before the government sees it. He has since given a reason for the app searches (section 13.5). Section 14 covers the provider's help pages and the record of the habit.
7. **Timestamp errors in a deck that depends on timestamps.** Slide 2 lists the re-contact as 15:47 (log: 15:49:15) and the image searches as 16:42, 16:47 and 16:50 (the screenshot shows 4:54 and 4:58 PM). The two-day timeline corrects both, so slide 2 should be withdrawn. Slide 9 still lists "will be down" as 16:56:57 (log: 16:58:57).
8. **"Physically impossible" overstates a good point.** "My reply was already on its way" is enough.
9. **Late arrival.** None of this was presented at the Article 32 hearing. That is his right. But the one interview statement relayed so far says he thought she needed money. Unless the video also has him saying "I thought it was someone else" or "I looked her up and she was 23," expect the argument that the deck's accounts were built afterwards from the phone records.
10. **Entrapment and voluntary abandonment are weak.** He re-initiated, stayed in the lot and identified his car.

## 4. How a panel or jury is likely to view the evidence

**Who decides**

- **Court-martial.** Military judge alone, or a panel of members senior to him (enlisted members if he requests). Panels tend to follow instructions on burden and intent carefully and to hold NCOs to a high standard. Conviction takes three-fourths, not unanimity, so one holdout does not help, but there is no hung panel either.
- **Bell County.** Twelve jurors, unanimous, in a military community. Texas Penal Code § 43.021(b)(2) makes solicitation a second-degree felony if the person was younger than 18, was *represented* to him as younger than 18, or was believed by him to be younger than 18. If he knew on May 1 that he was texting the person who had said "16 in june," the "represented" branch may be met whatever the image search led him to believe. The DPS draft cites § 43.031(b)(2), which looks like the wrong section for a buyer. Texas counsel should confirm what is actually charged.

## 5. If he testifies

**The decision.** It is his, made with counsel. For: a state-of-mind defense is hard to mount without him. Against: cross-examination on every text and on the interview, the prior patronizing, and the fact that sworn testimony in one forum can be used in the other (the Army and Texas are separate sovereigns). A mock cross-examination with counsel should come first.

**Ground rule.** He should testify only to what he actually remembers and believed. I will not suggest wording for anything untrue, and nothing loses faster than an account the extraction or the video contradicts. Where the deck reconstructs rather than remembers, the honest answer is "I don't remember," and the forensic examiner carries the point.

**Points to make, in this order, if they are true**

1. **Own the bad fact first.** "I was trying to pay an adult for sex. It was wrong and I knew it was illegal." Counsel should weigh conceding the Spec 3 conduct; it buys credibility for the fight that matters.
2. **April 30, plainly.** He answered an ad showing an adult. When told "16 in June," he said no and meant it.
3. **The image search.** What he did and the one real reason he did it. That includes how much of the profile he looked at (section 12.1).
4. **May 1.** How many ads he contacted, where this number came from, what was on the screen when he typed, and what he truly believed about who he was texting. The first draft of this point had him say he did not know it was the person from the day before. The relayed interview statement points the other way. If he did know, that line cannot be used, and his testimony has to start from what he told the interviewer (section 8). The defense position is now that the message was meant for a woman he had texted earlier that day (section 10). If that is what happened, this is where he says it, and the phone records have to carry it.
5. **The parking lot.** He was nervous about police, robbery and exposure because buying sex is illegal, not because of age.
6. **The interview.** Only after watching it. He will have to explain "she just needed money" and "just giving money and leaving" in his own words. If he never mentioned the image search that night, he will also have to say why. Section 13.2 assesses the explanation since offered.

**Claims that will cost credibility.** Keep one only if it is a real memory, and expect it to be attacked.

- "I did not intend to meet anyone."
- "Physically impossible."
- Reading "on" as "or."
- Carplay as a "bluff test."
- "Indifferent" to whether the encounter was genuine.
- Addiction as the explanation for May 1.

## 6. Likely cross-examination

Each entry gives the question, an honest shape for the answer, the prosecutor's rebuttal, and the reply. Every answer is conditional on being true. Answers 2 to 5 assume he did not know on May 1 that he was texting the person from April 30. The interview statement in section 8 suggests he did. If he did, those answers are not available to him. Section 10.6 lists the questions the later account will draw. Section 11.6 adds those raised by what he remembered of the profile. Section 12.4 adds those raised by the college email and the unsaved number. Section 13.4 assesses the answers he has since given to the questions in 12.4. Section 14.4 adds those raised by the provider's help pages and the record of the habit.

1. **"On April 30 you were told she was 15, and you understood?"**
   - Answer: "Yes. That's why I said no."
   - Rebuttal: "Then 'Not in rude way.' You kept the door open."
   - Reply: "No. I had just told a kid to change her life and didn't want to sound cruel."
2. **"23 hours later, same number: 'Are you still around?' 'Still,' because you had talked before."**
   - Answer: "I texted that number. I did not know it was the same one. That is how I open with ads; I sent the same kind of line to other numbers."
   - Rebuttal: "Same phone, same app. Your 'No' was on the screen above it."
   - Reply: depends on forensics. Either "The thread was gone; the screen was blank," or "I don't remember seeing it. I delete those threads. Had I seen it I would not have sent that."
   - Rebuttal: "You don't remember deleting it."
   - Reply: "Not that specific thread, no." Concede it and stop.
   - Update: the account now given is that he opened this conversation by mistake from a list of unsaved numbers (section 12.2). On that account "the thread was gone" cannot be the reply. What remains is what he remembers seeing, and what the examiner can show the screen displayed. He has since said he does not recall seeing his earlier words (section 13.4). The provider says a deleted conversation cannot be recovered, which also rules out "the thread was gone" (section 14.2).
3. **"Where did you get the number on May 1? The same ad you reverse-searched the day before?"**
   - He must know this cold: which site, which ad, what he recognized.
   - Rebuttal: "The only reason you searched that photo was that she said she was 15."
   - Reply: whatever the true reason was. This is the hardest link in the mistaken-identity account.
4. **"You check photos and demand verification, but you didn't notice the number?"**
   - Answer: "I check pictures, not numbers, and I had texted [number] ads that week." The count must come from the extraction.
   - If the conversations were listed by number only, with no names, he can say that too (section 12.2).
5. **"You never asked her age on May 1."**
   - Answer: "I had no reason to think it was an issue. The one time it was, I ended it in under a minute."
   - Rebuttal: "Or you already knew."
6. **"'2 for 1 tonight 100.' 'Ok. Where at?' You agreed to pay $100 for two girls."**
   - Answer, if true: "I had asked if I could send an Uber. She said no, they had a room. I said ok and asked where it was. I was not answering the price."
   - Rebuttal: "You read the price. You never said no to it. At the hotel you asked 'Cash right?' and offered extra."
   - Reply: concede what is true, that he did not object and later talked about cash, and stop. He should not claim he did not understand "2 for 1."
   - Follow-up: "Why did you want to know where the room was?" The answer has to be the truth, and it has to fit everything else he says about meeting her.
7. **"'u gonna cum on both of us lol.' 'Yes.'"**
   - Answer: "I had just said it was 20 minutes away. I read the first line as 'are you coming' and answered yes. The second line arrived two seconds before mine posted."
   - Rebuttal: "Then you wrote 'Just you….then both.' You never corrected it."
   - Reply: "By then I understood she meant both of them. I was saying who I wanted to see." That concedes intent toward an adult, not knowledge of age.
8. **"You drove 20 minutes and asked 'Cash right?'"**
   - Answer: "Yes. To meet an adult."
   - If he claims scam testing: "Then why drive there? Why offer extra?"
9. **"'I'm a little nervous.' Nervous because she was 15."**
   - Answer: "Nervous because buying sex is illegal, could end my career, and could have been a robbery or police."
   - Rebuttal: "You searched for stings and burner apps that day."
   - Reply: "For the same reason." He must know the exact search terms before trial. He has since given a different reason for the app search (section 13.5), so this reply now fits only the sting search.
10. **"You offered a 15-year-old extra money for sex in a car with tinted windows."**
    - Answer, if true: "I was afraid to go into that room, so I asked her to come to the car. I believed she was an adult."
    - The bluff version draws: "A bluff with more money? And when she agreed, you described your car."
11. **"You removed the app in the parking lot. You were destroying evidence."**
    - Answer: "I delete that app and those threads out of shame, and had many times before." This needs forensic proof from before April 30 (section 13.1). He has since said the Google activity shows it for the app. On his own answers the April 30 conversations were still in the list on May 1, so the answer should not include deleting threads that day unless the records show it (section 14.3).
    - Rebuttal: "But you didn't drive away."
    - Reply: the truth about why.
12. **"That night you told the agent: 'I refused and thought the next day she just needed money.' 'She' is the girl who told you she was 15?"**
    - Answer only after watching the video, and only the truth. "The question had no time frame" no longer works, because the sentence supplies its own: "the next day."
    - If he says he did not know it was her: "Then who is 'she'? Who 'just needed money'?"
    - If he says he knew but believed she was 23: "Where on this recording do you tell the agent she was 23?"
13. **"You say you thought about just giving her money and leaving. Then why 'Just you….then both'? Why 'Cash right?' Why 'Ill give extra' for the car?"**
    - No answer shape can be offered here. Whatever he says has to fit all three texts.
    - Follow-up: "And why give money and leave at all, unless you knew she was someone you should not touch?"
14. **"Your memory failed a few hours after it happened, but it is reliable today?"**
    - Answer, if true: "I was exhausted and frightened that night and I described it badly. For what I did, I am pointing to the phone records, not to my memory."
    - Rebuttal: "The records show what you looked at. Only you can say what you believed, and that night you said she needed money."
15. **"That same night, in over an hour, you never said you thought it was someone else, and you never said you had looked her up."**
    - The video controls. If he said either, counsel plays it. If he said neither, this may be the hardest question in the case.
16. **"The woman in the photos is 23, but you asked for a peace sign 'so I know real.' You thought the photos were fake, so they told you nothing."**
    - This needs one consistent truth about what he believed the photos showed.
17. **"How many times have you paid for sex?"** (if the judge allows it)
    - Answer: short and truthful. "With adults. Never a minor, and when one came up I refused."
18. **"You say addiction. So the urge overrode your judgment, as it overrode Thursday's 'no'?"**
    - Answer: "It made me careless about who I was texting. It has never made me want a child."

## 7. Before any decision

1. **Watch the full interview with counsel.** Transcribe every statement about age, identity, scam belief and intent. Section 8.2 lists what to look for around the "needed money" statement. Get the Army CID interview record as well; the DPS report says CID questioned him separately.
2. **Get the forensic report before fixing a theory.** Thread deletion time; app install and uninstall times; how the UC number was entered on May 1; the page viewed at 15:45–15:49; the number of ads contacted April 28 to May 1; exact search queries; the date of the Play Store screenshot. Section 12.2 adds tests for the accident account. Section 13.7 lists records that would support the newer details.
3. **Demand in discovery:** the ad (text, stated age, photos, site), the operation plan and any age-confirmation protocol, the full UC extraction with metadata, and the arrest inventory (cash on hand).
4. **Settle on the one true account and correct the timestamps.**
5. **Motions for counsel to assess:** suppression of the interview (the DPS report describes a joint DPS–Army operation, which raises an Article 31(b) question), M.R.E. 707, M.R.E. 404(b) on prior patronizing, the rule of completeness for the interview, and speedy trial under R.C.M. 707 (charges were preferred 24 June 2026).
6. **Coordinate military and Texas counsel.** Testimony or a guilty plea in one forum is usable in the other. The PHO's suggested deal does not say which specifications; a plea to Spec 1 or 2 would almost certainly bring sex-offender registration.
7. **Ask counsel about a psychosexual evaluation,** which is often used in sting cases for negotiation and sentencing.
8. **Keep work product inside the defense team.** Material shared outside the attorney-client relationship may not be privileged, and anything handed to a testifying expert can reach the prosecutor. **Points for counsel** (my understanding; each needs confirming)

- **Completeness.** If the government offers part of the interview, M.R.E. 304(h)(2) lets the defense introduce the remaining portions.

### 8.3 "The slide deck's explanations contradict each other," explained

**Why this outweighs any single exhibit.** Specs 1 and 2 are decided by what was in his mind on May 1. The texts show what he did. The only evidence of an innocent belief is his own account, so the case rests on whether he is believed. Someone who gives several accounts that cannot all be true is usually believed on none of them, including the parts that are true. Prosecutors also argue that shifting explanations show consciousness of guilt.

**The deck gives six answers to one question.** The question is what he believed and intended on May 1.

| Name | The answer | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Wrong person | I did not realize it was the number from the day before | Slides 2(e), 3, 4 |
| Adult | My search showed she was 23 | Slide 1 |
| Scam | I believed nobody real was there, and I was testing | Slides 7, 8, 9 |
| No intent | I did not intend to meet anyone | Slide 9 |
| Misread | The texts do not mean what they appear to say | Slides 5, 6, 7, 9 |
| Compulsion | I kept going without thinking | Slides 4, 7, 8 |

The interview adds a seventh: I knew who she was, felt sorry for her, and thought about giving money and leaving. The request adds an eighth: what I said in the interview was poor recall.

**Where they collide**

1. **Wrong person against Adult.** The search was run on the photo of the person who said "16 in june," within about a quarter of an hour of her saying it. Its result could shape what he believed on May 1 only if he knew he was texting that person. If he did not know, the result is irrelevant. If he did know, Wrong person is false. Slide 2(e) tries to keep both: he recognized the picture as the one that led to the adult profile, but not as the one whose owner had said she was 15. That asks the panel to accept that, 23 hours later, he remembered the answer and forgot the question.
2. **Wrong person against itself.** Slide 3 says the text was meant for someone else. Slide 2(e) says he meant to contact that ad. See 8.1, point 5 under "What it does not do." The defense has since chosen the slide 3 version (section 10), so slide 2(e) should be withdrawn.
3. **Adult against Scam.** To believe the texter was the 23-year-old in the photos, he had to believe the photos were hers. Scam says he had identified the photos as fake, and his own text asks for a peace-sign picture "so I know real." If the photos were not hers, they told him nothing about her age.
4. **Scam and No intent against what he did.** A man who thinks he is texting a scammer and does not intend to meet anyone does not drive 20 minutes, ask "Cash right?", ask to be walked upstairs and offer extra for the car. Slide 8 also calls him "indifferent" to whether the encounter was genuine, which is different from believing it was fake.
5. **No intent against Adult.** Adult explains why meeting her would not be the charged crime. No intent denies wanting to meet at all. A man who believed he was meeting an adult has no need for the second, and offering both suggests neither is firm.
6. **"Give money and leave" against the rest.** Slide 9 says that when signs pointed to a real person he "thought maybe I should only give money and leave." The interview says much the same. That thought makes little sense toward an adult escort or a scammer. It makes sense toward someone he believed he should not touch, which is what the other slides deny.
7. **The misreadings.** There are three, and each falls on exactly the message that hurts: "on" read as "or," "will" read as "we'll," and "Ok" answering only the earlier line. One misreading is believable. Three in a row, all in his favor, look like explanations written afterwards. They also leave the problem in place: on slide 7's own account, "Just you….then both" was his answer to the two-girl offer.
8. **Slide 1's argument turns around.** It says that if he had known of the previous day's age, he would have asked a question to clear up the discrepancy. The interview indicates he did know who she was, and he asked nothing. On the deck's own reasoning, that silence now counts against him.
9. **The interview against Wrong person and Scam.** "She" is the same person on both days, and nobody feels sorry for a scammer.

**How it plays in cross-examination.** Each collision becomes a question with no good answer, such as "Which is it: you didn't know it was her, or you knew and thought she was 23?" After two or three of those, the panel stops listening to the parts that are true and that help him: the refusal, and a history that involved adults only.

**The way out.** One account, and it has to be the true one. A workable test: it can be told in three or four sentences; it matches every text, every phone record and the video; and every exhibit the defense offers supports it. Anything that does not fit is dropped, however appealing on its own. Because the interview statement came first, the account has to begin with it, not go around it.

### 11.3 The question: why the search is not treated as a disclosure that she was an adult

**The answer in three parts**

1. **It has not been treated as anything, because it is not yet in any record.** The PHO's findings rest on the government's packet. The defense offered no evidence at the hearing. As far as the relayed interview shows, he did not mention the search to the agents. Before it can be weighed it has to reach the people who decide: the prosecutors, in negotiation, and the fact-finder, through the forensic examiner. When and how is for counsel.
2. **"Knew" is not the test in the court-martial, and that helps him.** Being told is easy to prove. But the charges are attempts, and an attempt is judged on the circumstances as the accused believed them to be (Manual for Courts-Martial, pt. IV, ¶ 4.c.(3)). In sting cases the specification usually reads "a person he believed to be a child" (for example *United States v. Axe*, N-M. Ct. Crim. App. 2020). The charge sheet here should be checked for that wording. So, on my understanding, the government has to prove more than that he was told. It has to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that on May 1 he believed she was under 16. The text is evidence that he did. The search is evidence that he did not. Neither is the belief itself.
3. **So the search does not have to cancel the text. It has to leave a reasonable doubt.** That is a lower bar than the question sets, and it is the better way to put the point.

**Why a fact-finder will not weigh the two equally**

| Point of comparison | The text | The profile |
|---|---|---|
| Form | An express, written statement of age | An express, written statement of age, supported by a dated college record |
| Who is speaking | The person he was dealing with | A third party with no part in the exchange |
| About whom | Herself | The profile's owner, the woman in the ad's photograph |
| What has to be assumed before it gives the texter's age | Nothing | That the person texting was the profile's owner |
| What he did on receiving it | Refused within 37 seconds, which shows he believed it | Nothing yet shown: no message, note or statement |
| When | 16:40 on April 30 | 16:54 on April 30, 11 minutes after the conversation ended |

1. **The row on what has to be assumed is the weak point.** When a photograph in an online ad traces to a private person's account in another state, the ordinary conclusion is that the photograph was borrowed. A borrowed photograph says nothing about the age of whoever placed the ad. His own conduct points to that conclusion: the next day he asked for a picture "so I know real," and slide 8 says he had identified the first photo as fake.
2. **The government will concede that the profile's owner is an adult.** It costs them nothing. The profile states an age, dates from July 2015 and carries a college record from 2021 (section 12.1). Sting operations commonly use pictures of adults. Their case is about the age the person texting stated, not about the woman in the pictures. Section 13.3 takes up the point that the profile states an age and is not only a picture.
3. **Courts have upheld convictions on this pattern.**
   - *United States v. Fulsom* (N-M. Ct. Crim. App. 2026): the persona was listed as 18 on an adult dating app and said by text that she was "almos 15." Convictions for attempted sexual assault and attempted sexual abuse of a child were affirmed.
   - *Perales v. State*, 622 S.W.3d 575 (Tex. App. 2021): the profile said 18, the defendant said the photos made him think she was about 23, and she told him by text that she was 16. The court held the jury could infer that he believed the text.
4. **Both cases differ from this one in a way that helps him.** In each, the accused was told the younger age and carried on with the conversation. In *Fulsom* the agents also gave him chances to back out, which he did not take. Here he took the chance on April 30, and the excerpts show no mention of age on May 1. That contrast is the defense's best point on belief, and it does not depend on the search.

**What would give the search more weight**

1. The second photo, if it is a verification photo of the same woman (11.2).
2. Any record made before the arrest that shows what he concluded from the search.
3. The advertisement. If it gave an adult age, the government's own statements to him conflicted. That is a stronger form of the argument than a third party's profile.
4. The video, if he mentions the search.

**Its limits**

1. **It has force only if the profile was in his mind on May 1.** There are three versions of that day.

   | Version | Did he know who she was? | What the search does |
   |---|---|---|
   | The government's | Yes, and he remembered "15" | Nothing. He went ahead anyway |
   | He knew, and the search had changed his mind | Yes, but he had concluded she was 23 | It is the reason for an adult belief |
   | His account, with point 3 | No, but he recognized the woman on the profile | It is the only age he connected with her |

   In a version where he neither knew who she was nor recognized the picture, the search plays no part. So "the search negated the disclosure" and "I did not know it was her" can be said together only in the third version, which rests on the split memory.
2. **Counsel can argue in the alternative. He cannot testify in the alternative.** A lawyer may tell the fact-finder: "He did not know it was her. If you think he did, the last thing he had learned about her was that she was 23." From the witness stand he can give only one account. The search itself can come in through the examiner without his testimony. What he concluded from it can come only from him.
3. **Doubt about her age may not be enough.** If the search left him unsure, the government may ask for a "deliberate avoidance" instruction: a person who suspects a fact and deliberately avoids confirming it can be treated as knowing it. Military courts recognize the instruction but limit when it can be given. *United States v. Brown*, 50 M.J. 262 (C.A.A.F. 1999), found it was error to give it on the facts there. Slide 1's point that he asked no questions on May 1 invites it. Whether it is available on the belief element of an attempt is a question for counsel.
4. **Genuine or reasonable, and whose burden.** For the completed offense, a mistake about age is a defense only if it was reasonable, and the accused has to prove it (Article 120b(d)(2); R.C.M. 916(j)(2)). For elements that require specific intent, R.C.M. 916(j)(1) says a mistake "need only have existed in the mind of the accused." Under the first rule he would have to prove that an adult belief was reasonable. Under the second, a genuine belief is enough and the government has to disprove it. I could not confirm which a military judge would apply to an attempt where there was no child. The search supports a genuine belief more easily than a reasonable one, so counsel should settle this first. Section 12.1 cites a case stating the general rule.

### 11.4 Texas

1. **The statute has three branches.** Texas Penal Code § 43.021(b)(2) applies if the person was (A) younger than 18, (B) "represented to the actor as being younger than 18," or (C) "believed by the actor to be younger than 18." The State can charge (B). A 2026 court of appeals decision upheld the evidence on a charge worded that way (*Brown v. State*, No. 06-25-00151-CR, Tex. App., Texarkana).
2. **On branch (B), the search probably changes nothing.** Under that branch the text is not evidence of what he knew. It is the thing the statute asks about: what was represented to him. The statement was made, and the person who made it never withdrew it. What he later concluded from another source goes to branch (C), which the State does not need.
3. **Two arguments remain for Texas counsel.**
   - The advertisement. If it gave an adult age, was she "represented" as under 18 when the representations conflicted?
   - Identity. If he did not know he was dealing with the person who had made the statement, does branch (B) still apply (section 10.8, point 4)?
4. **On belief, a Texas jury is free to reject this kind of argument,** as *Perales* shows.
5. **In Texas the search may be worth more for negotiation and punishment than for guilt.**

### 11.5 What this changes

1. **How to use the search.** Not as a disclosure that cancels the text. As a dated, pre-arrest reason to doubt that he believed she was under 16, proved by the examiner and argued by counsel.
2. **The belief defense has a core that depends on neither step.** The one time he believed she was a minor, he refused. Everything he was shown afterwards was an adult. Age was not mentioned on May 1. The accident, if proved, adds an explanation for the whole afternoon. Point 3, if it is what he remembers, adds one for the period after the second photo.
3. **A plea.** A military judge will accept a guilty plea only if the accused admits under oath the facts that make him guilty. There is no plea that maintains innocence (Article 45, UCMJ; R.C.M. 910). If his position is that he never believed she was under 16, he cannot plead guilty to Spec 1 or 2. Spec 3 is different, because age is not part of it. So the disposition the PHO suggested depends on which specifications it would cover. Texas does allow a no-contest plea, which is a matter for Texas counsel.
4. **Two urgent steps, both through counsel.**
   - The texting provider's records (11.1).
   - A capture of the profile as it stands, and any archived copy from about April 30. Profiles are edited and deleted. He should not contact the account holder.
5. **Discovery.** The advertisement, and where its photographs came from. Whether the operation's plan required the agent to restate the age or offer a way out, and whether either was done on May 1.
6. **The written record.** A federal court ruled in February 2026 that a defendant's exchanges with an AI tool, made on his own initiative, were protected by neither attorney-client privilege nor the work-product doctrine (*United States v. Heppner*, S.D.N.Y.). That is one court, and it does not bind a court-martial or a Texas court. But it describes this document and the conversation behind it. Counsel should be told that both exist.
