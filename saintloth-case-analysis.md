# United States v. SSG Saintloth: case feedback analysis

**Basis and limits.** Built from two documents only: the defense slide deck ("Details for DFE with messages") and the Preliminary Hearing Officer (PHO) report of 10 Aug 2026. Not reviewed: the interview video, the full chat extraction, the ad, any forensic report. I am not a lawyer and this is not legal advice. Statements of law are my understanding, and defense counsel should confirm each one.

**Updated 21 Nov 2026.** Section 8 adds a closer reading of the deck's timeline slide and one interview statement as it was relayed to me, not as heard on the video. Section 9 reviews the two-day timeline supplied afterwards and the "Ok. Where at?" point. Sections 1 to 7 have been revised where these change them.

## 1. Bottom line

- **Spec 3** (attempted patronizing, as amended) is strong for the government, and age is irrelevant to it. **Spec 4** should fall away: the PHO found no probable cause and the government said it would dismiss.
- **Specs 1 and 2** turn on one question: on May 1, did he actually believe the person he was texting was under 16? There was no real child, so these are attempts, and an attempt is judged on the facts as the accused believed them. "Should have realized" is not the test. In the excerpts provided, age is never mentioned on May 1.
- The government's answer: same number, same thread, 23 hours later, "Are you still around?"
- The defense's answer in the deck: the one time age came up he refused in 37 seconds, and the next day he lost track among many contacts.
- **That answer is now in serious doubt.** As relayed, he told the interviewer on May 1: "I refused and thought the next day she just needed money so the next day I thought about just giving money and leaving." That sentence treats the person he texted on May 1 as the person he refused on April 30. If the video matches it, "I didn't realize it was the same person" cannot be said, and the risk on Specs 1 and 2 is higher than the points above suggest (section 8).
- The slide deck weakens the defense, because it runs several explanations that contradict each other, the texts, and now the interview.
- Two things still have to be seen: the exact words on the interview video, with the question that drew them, and forensic proof of what was on his screen at 15:49:15 on May 1.

## 2. Prosecution

### Strengths

1. **Age was disclosed and understood.** "i will be 16 in june is that ok" drew "No. Find a different way of life please." He cannot claim he missed it.
2. **He initiated the return.** Same UC number, same thread, about 23 hours later. Nobody lured him back.
3. **May 1 shows intent to complete a paid encounter.** He offered an Uber, answered "Ok. Where at?" 18 seconds after "2 for 1 tonight 100," sent "Yes" and "Just you….then both," asked for pictures, drove about 20 minutes, asked "Cash right?", asked to be walked upstairs, wrote "I have tinted windows if you can carplay. Ill give extra," and identified his vehicle. Travel to the meeting place is a textbook substantial step.
4. **Concealment evidence, some of it supplied by the defense deck itself.** Tinted windows, nervousness, the peace-sign verification request, burner-number app searches, removal of the texting app in the parking lot, and a pre-arrival search for sting indicators.
5. **A recorded interview after a signed rights waiver.** DPS reports he "admitted to knowingly speaking to a minor," and he admitted prior patronizing. The relayed statement that he "thought the next day she just needed money" supplies the link the texts lack: that on May 1 he knew who he was texting (section 8).

### Weaknesses

1. **No ad in the evidence packet.** The government told the PHO he answered an ad for an underage child. The PHO rejected that for lack of evidence and found he did not know the age until told.
2. **Nothing about age on May 1.** The UC never re-confirmed age in the conversation that matters. The PHO found only that he "had reason to believe" she was 15, which is a probable-cause finding and not proof beyond a reasonable doubt of actual belief.
3. **The April 30 refusal is the government's own exhibit.** It was immediate and moral. The PHO's word "hesitated" undersells it.
4. **On May 1 the explicit words are the UC's.** His are "Yes," "Just you….then both" and "carplay." "An amazing bj" predates the age disclosure. Spec 1 (indecent language) depends on reading short replies in context.
5. **The "Yes" timing.** It posted 2 seconds after "on both of us lol" and 24 seconds after "ok u gonna cum," which followed his own "Its 20 min away from me." A "you gonna come?" reading is available.
6. **The PHO's intent reasoning is thin.** It infers specific intent "because" the conduct was a substantial step, and it treats the $50 agreement as if it came after the age disclosure. It came before, and he withdrew. At trial, intent must be proved on its own.
7. **The DPS summary of the "admission" is second-hand.** It comes from an agent who was not in the room, and the PHO, who watched the video, cited it only for prior patronizing "but not minors" and for marital status. If nothing on the video says he knew or believed on May 1 that she was 15, the summary overstates. This is a smaller weakness than it first appeared, because the relayed statement lets the government argue the same point from his own words (section 8).
8. **Polygraph evidence is inadmissible (M.R.E. 707),** which complicates how the interview is presented.

## 3. Defense

### Strengths

1. **The 37-second refusal.** It is direct evidence of what he does when he knows the age.
2. **The legal standard.** The government must prove what he believed, and its proof for May 1 is inference.
3. **Everything he saw was adult.** By his own search, the photos trace to a 23-year-old's account, and the PHO found his history was with adults, "not minors."
4. **Digital corroboration, if it holds.** The image search 11 minutes after the April 30 chat ended; other profiles messaged on May 1 before the UC (the two-day timeline lists five between 13:48 and 15:30, all still awaiting forensic confirmation); hotel lookups in Round Rock and Georgetown at 3:18 PM, half an hour before the UC text; the same stock opener to other numbers ("Are you still in killeen?", "Hi are you still available?").
5. **An innocent reading of the caution.** Paying an adult for sex is also a crime and a career-ender for an NCO. Nervousness, deletion and tinted windows do not distinguish between the two.
6. **The PHO's recommendation.** A neutral judge advocate recommended a bad-conduct discharge with no confinement. It is non-binding, but it is leverage in negotiation.

### Weaknesses

1. **Too many theories, and they fight each other.** A panel that hears all of them is likely to believe none. Section 8.3 explains this in full.
   - "I didn't realize it was the same person" conflicts with "my image search told me she was an adult." The second means he recognized the ad he had researched right after being told 15.
   - "The photos showed an adult" conflicts with "I knew the photos were fake." Fake photos say nothing about who is texting.
   - "I did not intend to meet anyone" conflicts with the drive, "Cash right?", "Can you walk me upstairs?" and "Ill give extra."
   - Three separate misreadings ("on" as "or," "will" as "we'll," "Ok" answering only "we have a room") look contrived when stacked.
2. **The linchpin is unproven.** The deck says he "can't definitively remember" deleting the April 30 thread. If the extraction shows the thread was still there at 15:49:15 on May 1, the refusal exchange sat directly above the box he typed in. Uninstalling the app cannot explain the mistake either, since the UC log shows the May 1 text arriving in the same thread from the same sender.
3. **His diligence cuts against him.** A man who reverse-searches photos, demands verification and checks for stings is hard to believe when he says he did not notice a number from 23 hours earlier.
4. **The image search began within minutes of the age disclosure.** Expect: "You were checking whether she was really 15, looking for a reason to say yes."
5. **"Compulsion" serves the government's story too:** the urge overrode Thursday's "no." It is mitigation for sentencing, not a defense to intent.
6. **Self-inflicted exhibits.** Searches for second-number apps at 2:01–2:04 PM on May 1, including a visit to "Vikk – AI Lawyer & Legal Help" at 2:02 PM; the claimed pre-arrival sting search; removal of the texting apps at 17:02, minutes after he described his car. Each needs a truthful explanation before the government sees it.
7. **Timestamp errors in a deck that depends on timestamps.** Slide 2 lists the re-contact as 15:47 (log: 15:49:15) and the image searches as 16:42, 16:47 and 16:50 (the screenshot shows 4:54 and 4:58 PM). The two-day timeline corrects both, so slide 2 should be withdrawn. Slide 9 still lists "will be down" as 16:56:57 (log: 16:58:57).
8. **"Physically impossible" overstates a good point.** "My reply was already on its way" is enough.
9. **Late arrival.** None of this was presented at the Article 32 hearing. That is his right. But the one interview statement relayed so far says he thought she needed money. Unless the video also has him saying "I thought it was someone else" or "I looked her up and she was 23," expect the argument that the deck's accounts were built afterwards from the phone records.
10. **Entrapment and voluntary abandonment are weak.** He re-initiated, stayed in the lot and identified his car.

## 4. How a panel or jury is likely to view the evidence

**Who decides**

- **Court-martial.** Military judge alone, or a panel of members senior to him (enlisted members if he requests). Panels tend to follow instructions on burden and intent carefully and to hold NCOs to a high standard. Conviction takes three-fourths, not unanimity, so one holdout does not help, but there is no hung panel either.
- **Bell County.** Twelve jurors, unanimous, in a military community. Texas Penal Code § 43.021(b)(2) makes solicitation a second-degree felony if the person was younger than 18, was *represented* to him as younger than 18, or was believed by him to be younger than 18. If he knew on May 1 that he was texting the person who had said "16 in june," the "represented" branch may be met whatever the image search led him to believe. The DPS draft cites § 43.031(b)(2), which looks like the wrong section for a buyer. Texas counsel should confirm what is actually charged.

## 5. If he testifies

**The decision.** It is his, made with counsel. For: a state-of-mind defense is hard to mount without him. Against: cross-examination on every text and on the interview, the prior patronizing, and the fact that sworn testimony in one forum can be used in the other (the Army and Texas are separate sovereigns). A mock cross-examination with counsel should come first.

**Ground rule.** He should testify only to what he actually remembers and believed. I will not suggest wording for anything untrue, and nothing loses faster than an account the extraction or the video contradicts. Where the deck reconstructs rather than remembers, the honest answer is "I don't remember," and the forensic examiner carries the point.

**Points to make, in this order, if they are true**

1. **Own the bad fact first.** "I was trying to pay an adult for sex. It was wrong and I knew it was illegal." Counsel should weigh conceding the Spec 3 conduct; it buys credibility for the fight that matters.
2. **April 30, plainly.** He answered an ad showing an adult. When told "16 in June," he said no and meant it.
3. **The image search.** What he did and the one real reason he did it.
4. **May 1.** How many ads he contacted, where this number came from, what was on the screen when he typed, and what he truly believed about who he was texting. The first draft of this point had him say he did not know it was the person from the day before. The relayed interview statement points the other way. If he did know, that line cannot be used, and his testimony has to start from what he told the interviewer (section 8).
5. **The parking lot.** He was nervous about police, robbery and exposure because buying sex is illegal, not because of age.
6. **The interview.** Only after watching it. He will have to explain "she just needed money" and "just giving money and leaving" in his own words. If he never mentioned the image search that night, he will also have to say why.

**Claims that will cost credibility.** Keep one only if it is a real memory, and expect it to be attacked.

- "I did not intend to meet anyone."
- "Physically impossible."
- Reading "on" as "or."
- Carplay as a "bluff test."
- "Indifferent" to whether the encounter was genuine.
- Addiction as the explanation for May 1.

## 6. Likely cross-examination

Each entry gives the question, an honest shape for the answer, the prosecutor's rebuttal, and the reply. Every answer is conditional on being true. Answers 2 to 5 assume he did not know on May 1 that he was texting the person from April 30. The interview statement in section 8 suggests he did. If he did, those answers are not available to him.

1. **"On April 30 you were told she was 15, and you understood?"**
   - Answer: "Yes. That's why I said no."
   - Rebuttal: "Then 'Not in rude way.' You kept the door open."
   - Reply: "No. I had just told a kid to change her life and didn't want to sound cruel."
2. **"23 hours later, same number: 'Are you still around?' 'Still,' because you had talked before."**
   - Answer: "I texted that number. I did not know it was the same one. That is how I open with ads; I sent the same kind of line to other numbers."
   - Rebuttal: "Same phone, same app. Your 'No' was on the screen above it."
   - Reply: depends on forensics. Either "The thread was gone; the screen was blank," or "I don't remember seeing it. I delete those threads. Had I seen it I would not have sent that."
   - Rebuttal: "You don't remember deleting it."
   - Reply: "Not that specific thread, no." Concede it and stop.
3. **"Where did you get the number on May 1? The same ad you reverse-searched the day before?"**
   - He must know this cold: which site, which ad, what he recognized.
   - Rebuttal: "The only reason you searched that photo was that she said she was 15."
   - Reply: whatever the true reason was. This is the hardest link in the mistaken-identity account.
4. **"You check photos and demand verification, but you didn't notice the number?"**
   - Answer: "I check pictures, not numbers, and I had texted [number] ads that week." The count must come from the extraction.
5. **"You never asked her age on May 1."**
   - Answer: "I had no reason to think it was an issue. The one time it was, I ended it in under a minute."
   - Rebuttal: "Or you already knew."
6. **"'2 for 1 tonight 100.' 'Ok. Where at?' You agreed to pay $100 for two girls."**
   - Answer, if true: "I had asked if I could send an Uber. She said no, they had a room. I said ok and asked where it was. I was not answering the price."
   - Rebuttal: "You read the price. You never said no to it. At the hotel you asked 'Cash right?' and offered extra."
   - Reply: concede what is true, that he did not object and later talked about cash, and stop. He should not claim he did not understand "2 for 1."
   - Follow-up: "Why did you want to know where the room was?" The answer has to be the truth, and it has to fit everything else he says about meeting her.
7. **"'u gonna cum on both of us lol.' 'Yes.'"**
   - Answer: "I had just said it was 20 minutes away. I read the first line as 'are you coming' and answered yes. The second line arrived two seconds before mine posted."
   - Rebuttal: "Then you wrote 'Just you….then both.' You never corrected it."
   - Reply: "By then I understood she meant both of them. I was saying who I wanted to see." That concedes intent toward an adult, not knowledge of age.
8. **"You drove 20 minutes and asked 'Cash right?'"**
   - Answer: "Yes. To meet an adult."
   - If he claims scam testing: "Then why drive there? Why offer extra?"
9. **"'I'm a little nervous.' Nervous because she was 15."**
   - Answer: "Nervous because buying sex is illegal, could end my career, and could have been a robbery or police."
   - Rebuttal: "You searched for stings and burner apps that day."
   - Reply: "For the same reason." He must know the exact search terms before trial.
10. **"You offered a 15-year-old extra money for sex in a car with tinted windows."**
    - Answer, if true: "I was afraid to go into that room, so I asked her to come to the car. I believed she was an adult."
    - The bluff version draws: "A bluff with more money? And when she agreed, you described your car."
11. **"You removed the app in the parking lot. You were destroying evidence."**
    - Answer: "I delete that app and those threads out of shame, and had many times before." This needs forensic proof from before May 1.
    - Rebuttal: "But you didn't drive away."
    - Reply: the truth about why.
12. **"That night you told the agent: 'I refused and thought the next day she just needed money.' 'She' is the girl who told you she was 15?"**
    - Answer only after watching the video, and only the truth. "The question had no time frame" no longer works, because the sentence supplies its own: "the next day."
    - If he says he did not know it was her: "Then who is 'she'? Who 'just needed money'?"
    - If he says he knew but believed she was 23: "Where on this recording do you tell the agent she was 23?"
13. **"You say you thought about just giving her money and leaving. Then why 'Just you….then both'? Why 'Cash right?' Why 'Ill give extra' for the car?"**
    - No answer shape can be offered here. Whatever he says has to fit all three texts.
    - Follow-up: "And why give money and leave at all, unless you knew she was someone you should not touch?"
14. **"Your memory failed a few hours after it happened, but it is reliable today?"**
    - Answer, if true: "I was exhausted and frightened that night and I described it badly. For what I did, I am pointing to the phone records, not to my memory."
    - Rebuttal: "The records show what you looked at. Only you can say what you believed, and that night you said she needed money."
15. **"That same night, in over an hour, you never said you thought it was someone else, and you never said you had looked her up."**
    - The video controls. If he said either, counsel plays it. If he said neither, this may be the hardest question in the case.
16. **"The woman in the photos is 23, but you asked for a peace sign 'so I know real.' You thought the photos were fake, so they told you nothing."**
    - This needs one consistent truth about what he believed the photos showed.
17. **"How many times have you paid for sex?"** (if the judge allows it)
    - Answer: short and truthful. "With adults. Never a minor, and when one came up I refused."
18. **"You say addiction. So the urge overrode your judgment, as it overrode Thursday's 'no'?"**
    - Answer: "It made me careless about who I was texting. It has never made me want a child."

## 7. Before any decision

1. **Watch the full interview with counsel.** Transcribe every statement about age, identity, scam belief and intent. Section 8.2 lists what to look for around the "needed money" statement. Get the Army CID interview record as well; the DPS report says CID questioned him separately.
2. **Get the forensic report before fixing a theory.** Thread deletion time; app install and uninstall times; how the UC number was entered on May 1; the page viewed at 15:45–15:49; the number of ads contacted April 28 to May 1; exact search queries; the date of the Play Store screenshot.
3. **Demand in discovery:** the ad (text, stated age, photos, site), the operation plan and any age-confirmation protocol, the full UC extraction with metadata, and the arrest inventory (cash on hand).
4. **Settle on the one true account and correct the timestamps.**
5. **Motions for counsel to assess:** suppression of the interview (the DPS report describes a joint DPS–Army operation, which raises an Article 31(b) question), M.R.E. 707, M.R.E. 404(b) on prior patronizing, the rule of completeness for the interview, and speedy trial under R.C.M. 707 (charges were preferred 24 June 2026).
6. **Coordinate military and Texas counsel.** Testimony or a guilty plea in one forum is usable in the other. The PHO's suggested deal does not say which specifications; a plea to Spec 1 or 2 would almost certainly bring sex-offender registration.
7. **Ask counsel about a psychosexual evaluation,** which is often used in sting cases for negotiation and sentencing.
8. **Keep work product inside the defense team.** Material shared outside the attorney-client relationship may not be privileged, and anything handed to a testifying expert can reach the prosecutor. Route the forensic examiner through counsel.

### 8.2 The interview statement

As relayed: "I refused and thought the next day she just needed money so the next day I thought about just giving money and leaving."

**What it gives the government**

1. **Identity.** "She" is one person across both days. The government no longer has to prove that the April 30 thread was still on his screen.
2. **Belief, by inference.** A man who thinks he is meeting an adult escort has little reason to hand over money and leave, and nobody feels sorry for a scammer. The thought fits best with someone he believed he should not touch.
3. **Continuity with his own text.** "Find a different way of life please" on April 30 and "she just needed money" in the interview show the same concern for the same person. That makes the statement look like memory, not confusion.
4. **Timing.** He said it the same day, before he had a lawyer and before he had seen the evidence. Fact-finders trust that kind of statement over a later one.
5. **A half they can disprove.** "Just giving money and leaving" runs into "Just you….then both," "Cash right?" and "Ill give extra." The government will ask the panel to accept the half that hurts him and reject the half that helps, and the texts allow it.

**What it gives the defense**

1. **It repeats the refusal and denies wanting sex with her.** If the government plays part of the interview, the defense can ask to bring in the rest.
2. **It may not contain the word "minor."** The DPS line that he "admitted to knowingly speaking to a minor" may be an agent's reading of this sentence. If nothing on the video says he knew or believed on May 1 that she was 15, counsel can show that the report overstates. The government will still play the clip and argue the inference, and it is the inference most listeners would draw.
3. **It shows conflict, not predation.** In his own words on the day: refusal, sympathy, second thoughts. With the PHO's finding that his history involved adults, "not minors," that is strong material for negotiation and sentencing even if it does not win Specs 1 and 2.

**Why "poor recall" is a weak explanation**

- The interview was on May 1. "The next day I thought about just giving money and leaving" describes that same afternoon.
- The error claimed is not a wrong time or a forgotten detail. It is describing as familiar a person he now says he did not recognize. People rarely get that wrong within hours.
- The deck concedes poor memory now (slide 3: "I can't definitively remember"; slide 10: "I don't remember saying"). The prosecutor will ask why his memory failed that evening but is reliable today.
- A more specific explanation would carry more weight, but only if the recording bears it out. The recording will show how long he had been questioned, whether the "needed money" idea came first from him or from the interviewer, and whether he was describing what he thought before the arrest or what he understood after being told whose number it was.

**Why "the phone records disprove it" claims too much**

- The records and the statement are not opposites. Both can be true: he saw the profile on April 30, and on May 1 he still thought of her as the girl who needed money.
- Where they pull apart, the defense says the statement is wrong and the government says the new theory is wrong. The government has his own same-day words on its side.
- If he believed that evening that she was 23, the natural thing to tell the agent was "I looked her up and she is 23." Whether he said that anywhere in the interview is now one of the most important facts in the case.
- "Disprove" invites the same damage as "physically impossible." What the records fairly support is narrower: the investigators' summary skipped an intervening event, and he had been shown that the photos belonged to an adult.

**What to look for on the video**

1. The exact words, and the question asked just before them.
2. Who first said "needed money," or anything like "help her": he or the interviewer.
3. Whether he mentions looking her up, a profile, "23," or believing she was an adult.
4. Whether he says anywhere that he did not realize it was the same number.
5. When he says the "give money and leave" thought came: before texting, on the drive, or in the parking lot. The timing matters, because the texts the PHO relied on for Spec 1 were sent at 15:59 to 16:00 and at 16:58, with the drive in between.
6. Whether the statement came before, during or after the polygraph, and how long he had been held by then.
7. The separate Army CID interview: any recording, notes or signed statement.

**Points for counsel** (my understanding; each needs confirming)

- **Completeness.** If the government offers part of the interview, M.R.E. 304(h)(2) lets the defense introduce the remaining portions.
- **Impeachment survives suppression.** Under M.R.E. 304(e), a statement excluded for an Article 31 or rights-warning violation can still be used to contradict the accused's own testimony. Winning a suppression motion would not make it safe to testify to something the statement contradicts.
- **Polygraph circumstances.** If the statement came out of the polygraph session, *United States v. Kohlbek* (C.A.A.F. 2019) held that M.R.E. 707 does not bar evidence about the circumstances of the examination when it is offered to explain why a statement was made.
- **Belief or suspicion.** The image search may show at most that he was unsure of her age. Whether the government must prove he actually believed she was under 16, or whether going ahead while unsure is enough, should be answered before a theory is chosen.
- **Texas.** § 43.021(b)(2) reaches a person "represented to the actor as being younger than 18." If he knew he was texting the person who had said "16 in june," what he came to believe afterwards may not matter there.

### 8.3 "The slide deck's explanations contradict each other," explained

**Why this outweighs any single exhibit.** Specs 1 and 2 are decided by what was in his mind on May 1. The texts show what he did. The only evidence of an innocent belief is his own account, so the case rests on whether he is believed. Someone who gives several accounts that cannot all be true is usually believed on none of them, including the parts that are true. Prosecutors also argue that shifting explanations show consciousness of guilt.

**The deck gives six answers to one question.** The question is what he believed and intended on May 1.

| Name | The answer | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Wrong person | I did not realize it was the number from the day before | Slides 2(e), 3, 4 |
| Adult | My search showed she was 23 | Slide 1 |
| Scam | I believed nobody real was there, and I was testing | Slides 7, 8, 9 |
| No intent | I did not intend to meet anyone | Slide 9 |
| Misread | The texts do not mean what they appear to say | Slides 5, 6, 7, 9 |
| Compulsion | I kept going without thinking | Slides 4, 7, 8 |

The interview adds a seventh: I knew who she was, felt sorry for her, and thought about giving money and leaving. The request adds an eighth: what I said in the interview was poor recall.

**Where they collide**

1. **Wrong person against Adult.** The search was run on the photo of the person who said "16 in june," within about a quarter of an hour of her saying it. Its result could shape what he believed on May 1 only if he knew he was texting that person. If he did not know, the result is irrelevant. If he did know, Wrong person is false. Slide 2(e) tries to keep both: he recognized the picture as the one that led to the adult profile, but not as the one whose owner had said she was 15. That asks the panel to accept that, 23 hours later, he remembered the answer and forgot the question.
2. **Wrong person against itself.** Slide 3 says the text was meant for someone else. Slide 2(e) says he meant to contact that ad. See 8.1, point 5 under "What it does not do."
3. **Adult against Scam.** To believe the texter was the 23-year-old in the photos, he had to believe the photos were hers. Scam says he had identified the photos as fake, and his own text asks for a peace-sign picture "so I know real." If the photos were not hers, they told him nothing about her age.
4. **Scam and No intent against what he did.** A man who thinks he is texting a scammer and does not intend to meet anyone does not drive 20 minutes, ask "Cash right?", ask to be walked upstairs and offer extra for the car. Slide 8 also calls him "indifferent" to whether the encounter was genuine, which is different from believing it was fake.
5. **No intent against Adult.** Adult explains why meeting her would not be the charged crime. No intent denies wanting to meet at all. A man who believed he was meeting an adult has no need for the second, and offering both suggests neither is firm.
6. **"Give money and leave" against the rest.** Slide 9 says that when signs pointed to a real person he "thought maybe I should only give money and leave." The interview says much the same. That thought makes little sense toward an adult escort or a scammer. It makes sense toward someone he believed he should not touch, which is what the other slides deny.
7. **The misreadings.** There are three, and each falls on exactly the message that hurts: "on" read as "or," "will" read as "we'll," and "Ok" answering only the earlier line. One misreading is believable. Three in a row, all in his favor, look like explanations written afterwards. They also leave the problem in place: on slide 7's own account, "Just you….then both" was his answer to the two-girl offer.
8. **Slide 1's argument turns around.** It says that if he had known of the previous day's age, he would have asked a question to clear up the discrepancy. The interview indicates he did know who she was, and he asked nothing. On the deck's own reasoning, that silence now counts against him.
9. **The interview against Wrong person and Scam.** "She" is the same person on both days, and nobody feels sorry for a scammer.

**How it plays in cross-examination.** Each collision becomes a question with no good answer, such as "Which is it: you didn't know it was her, or you knew and thought she was 23?" After two or three of those, the panel stops listening to the parts that are true and that help him: the refusal, and a history that involved adults only.

**The way out.** One account, and it has to be the true one. A workable test: it can be told in three or four sentences; it matches every text, every phone record and the video; and every exhibit the defense offers supports it. Anything that does not fit is dropped, however appealing on its own. Because the interview statement came first, the account has to begin with it, not go around it.

### 8.4 What this changes

1. **The risk on Specs 1 and 2 is higher than the first draft assessed.** The first draft treated "did he know who he was texting" as open. The relayed statement goes a long way toward closing it against him.
2. **The decision to testify is harder.** If he knew it was the same person, he cannot say otherwise on the stand, and the statement can be used against any testimony that contradicts it.
3. **The phone evidence is better used for a narrower purpose.** It supports "the investigators' summary is incomplete" and "he had reason to doubt she was 15." It does not support "he did not know who she was."
4. **Negotiation deserves a serious conversation with counsel.** The PHO recommended a bad-conduct discharge with no confinement. The refusal, the tone of the interview and the "not minors" finding carry weight in that setting. Which specifications a plea would cover, sex-offender registration and the Texas case all belong in that conversation. The decision is his.
5. **The first step is unchanged.** Watch the interview with counsel before committing to any account.

**The defense reading at its best.** He had asked whether he could send an Uber. She answered no, they had a room. "Ok. Where at?" completes that exchange: fine, where is it? On this reading "Ok" answers her reply to his own question, and the price line is an upsell he did not respond to. That is a coherent reading, and what a short text meant is a question of fact for the panel or jury.

**Why most fact-finders will still read it as going along with the offer**

1. It came 18 seconds after the price, which is long enough to read it. The 2-second gap before "Yes" does real work. This gap does not.
2. He did not question or refuse the price or the second girl, then or later.
3. What followed points the same way: "Just you….then both," which slide 7 says answered "2 for 1 tonight 100"; "Do guys have pictures?"; "Cash right?"; "Ill give extra."
4. He had paid for sex before (PHO finding 4g), so slide 5's claim that "2 for 1 didn't make sense until later" will not be believed.

**What the hotel search adds.** It shows that "where" was a real question about location, which nobody disputes. It does not help decide what "Ok" answered, because on both readings he wanted to know where the room was. It also works for the government: looking up the hotel, saying "Its 20 min away from me" and driving there is the planning of the trip. And it sits badly with slide 7, which says the address is what made him think it was a scam.

**What winning the point would be worth.** Little.

- It would correct PHO finding 4d, that he agreed to pay $100 for both, as to the moment of 15:53:50.
- It would not remove agreement to pay. "Cash right?" and "Ill give extra" show that on their own, and "extra" assumes a price already understood. Spec 3 and the Texas charge do not need "Ok."
- It does not touch Specs 1 and 2, which turn on who he believed she was.
- The reading concedes that he wanted to know where the room was. The next question is "Why?", and the answer collides with "I did not intend to meet anyone."

**How to use it.** As a narrow, accurate correction: the message is ambiguous, and the PHO treated it as a firm agreement to a price. Say it once and move on. It should not be paired with "I didn't understand 2 for 1," and it should not be offered as one of three misreadings (section 8.3, point 7). If the Spec 3 conduct is conceded for credibility (section 5, point 1), this point may not be worth raising at all.
